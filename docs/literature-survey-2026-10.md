# Minoan Language and Linear A: Phase 1 Literature Survey for a Falsifiable Computational Research Programme (status as of October 2026)

> Repo note: dated snapshot, copied from the claude.ai project on 2026-11-04. Do not update in place; corrections go in `research-notes.md`. Section 9 is superseded as the working plan by `plans/phase2-analysis-plan.md`.

The best place for an early computational result that can be published and defended is not decipherment. It is (a) internal, constraint-based analysis of the Linear A administrative corpus (arithmetic, fractions, morphology, sign-value consistency), and (b) building null-model benchmarks that measure whether any "language-matching" method has the power to work at Linear A's corpus size. The language behind Linear A ("Minoan") is still unidentified. The only well-supported conclusions are structural ones: the script is logo-syllabic, the number system is decimal, the language was probably agglutinative and prefixing with verb-initial syntax, and a few dozen words, names and place names can be read or interpreted. Ancient DNA shows that Neopalatial Cretans had essentially no steppe ancestry before about the 17th century BC. That makes Greek or any other steppe-derived Indo-European language very unlikely for Minoan. It does not exclude a non-Indo-European language, nor an Anatolian-related one.

## TL;DR

- **What is known with near-certainty:** Linear A is a logo-syllabic script (some signs stand for syllables, others for whole words) and is the parent of Linear B. Most syllabic signs can be read approximately using Linear B values. Numbers are decimal and fractions are additive. The corpus is tiny: the Linear A Digital Corpus (Perono Cacciafoco group) counts 1,427 documents with 7,362–7,396 signs, mostly administrative and about half of it from Hagia Triada. KU-RO = "total" is certain. A small set of place names (PA-I-TO = Phaistos, SU-KI-RI-TA = Sybrita, SE-TO-I-JA) and personal names are shared with Linear B. Minoan was probably agglutinative, used prefixes heavily, and put the verb first (VSO). No published decipherment has been accepted.
- **Indo-European or not:** the mainstream view is that Minoan is unclassified and probably non-Indo-European. The Anatolian/Luwian hypothesis (Palmer, Finkelberg) is a serious minority position. Semitic (Gordon, Best, and in 2026 di Mino), Greek, Indo-Iranian and Uralic (Revesz) proposals are fringe or rejected. Ancient DNA (Lazaridis 2017 and 2022, Clemente 2021, Skourtanioti 2023) shows Minoans were mostly Neolithic Aegean–Anatolian farmers with some Caucasus/Iran-related and Anatolian Early Bronze Age input. Per Skourtanioti et al. 2023, steppe-linked ancestry "appeared in Crete gradually from the seventeenth to twelfth centuries bc", and the earliest sampled individuals (late 17th/16th-century BC Aposelemis) have minimal or none. This argues strongly against Greek and Indo-Iranian but cannot decide between non-Indo-European and Anatolian Indo-European.
- **Where to start computationally:** (1) a replicable arithmetic and fraction-constraint analysis on SigLA/GORILA data; (2) affix and alternation discovery tested against shuffled-corpus null models; (3) a "decipherability power analysis", i.e. a published benchmark showing whether cognate-matching methods can recover known language pairs (Linear B→Greek, Ugaritic→Hebrew) once degraded to Linear A's size and noise and compared against random lexicons. The user's existing null-model test of di Mino's root matching is exactly the right seed for (3).

## 1. Notation and concepts (primer for non-specialists)

- **Logo-syllabic script.** Linear A mixes **syllabograms** (signs for open syllables: V or CV, e.g. *a*, *ku*, *ro*) with **logograms/ideograms** (signs for whole words or commodities, e.g. WINE, OLIVE OIL, MAN), **numerals**, and **klasmatograms** (fraction or measure signs). Signs can be fused into **ligatures** and **composite signs**; most composite signs occur only once (*hapax legomena*). (Consensus; Wikipedia "Linear A" summarising Salgarella 2025 and Tomas.)
- **Transliteration conventions.** By convention Linear A is written in **capitals with hyphens between syllables** (KU-RO). Linear B is written in lower-case italics (*ku-ro*, *to-so*). A sign with no agreed sound value is written with an asterisk and its catalogue number (*301). Homophones and less certain variants get subscripts (pa₂, ra₂ = "rya", ta₂ = "tya"). Logograms get **Latin capitals** (VIN = wine, OLE = oil, GRA = grain/wheat, OLIV = olives, FIC = figs, OVIS = sheep, CAP = goat, SUS = pig, BOS = cattle, VIR = man, TELA = cloth). Ligatures are written with "+" (e.g. MA+RU). Fractions are written as capital letters (J, E, B, D, F, K…). Erasures are marked ⟦…⟧.
- **Sign numbering.** Numbering follows **Emmett L. Bennett Jr.'s** system as adopted in GORILA. **AB nn** (e.g. AB 01 = *da*, AB 08 = *a*) marks a sign shared by Linear A and Linear B. **A nnn**, typically *301 upward (e.g. *301–*306), marks signs found only in Linear A. Logograms and vessel signs have their own ranges (e.g. *400–*418 VAS for vessel types). Unicode encodes Linear A at U+10600–U+1077F (added in Unicode 7.0, 2014). (Consensus; Wikipedia "Linear A".)
- **Homomorphy–homophony principle.** If two related scripts share a sign shape (homomorphy), the sign probably had a similar sound (homophony). This is how Linear B values are carried back to Linear A. It is supported by (i) about 10 core signs whose values agree across Linear B and the later Cypriot syllabary, which descends from Linear A through Cypro-Minoan; (ii) place and personal names shared between Linear A and Linear B; (iii) alternations such as A-/JA- that make phonological sense under Linear B values. (Mainstream; Steele 2024, Salgarella 2020, as summarised in Wikipedia.) Estimates of how far Linear A values drifted from Linear B include Packard 1974: 10.80 ± 1.80%; Duhoux 1989: 14.34 ± 1.80%; Owens 1996: 9–13% (cited in Owens 1999). These numbers rest on assumptions and should be treated as orders of magnitude, not measurements.
- **"Partially deciphered".** We can *pronounce* most Linear A words approximately, but in most cases we cannot *understand* them. That is the gap a decipherment would close.

## 2. Corpus, editions and digital resources

**Corpus size and composition (Consensus/High)**
- The Linear A Digital Corpus (Perono Cacciafoco group) counts **1,427 documents with 7,362–7,396 signs**; Salgarella told the Athens-Macedonia News Agency in 2021 that there are "only around 1,400 inscriptions in Linear A script", against "about 6,000" for Linear B. About 1,100 more objects carry apparent Linear A signs but are too damaged to read. John Younger noted that the whole corpus fills **only 1.84 letter-size pages** in 12-point type. Linear B, by comparison, has about 6,000 inscriptions. (Wikipedia citing Salgarella 2025; Salgarella, Aeon.)
- **Hagia Triada** dominates: about half of all inscribed objects (1,269 objects with writing, including sealings) and **147 tablets**. Next come Zakros (595 objects; 31 tablets), Khania (313; 99 tablets), Phaistos (65; 26 tablets), Knossos (32) and Palaikastro (23). (Salgarella 2025, via Wikipedia.) Most surviving documents are **LM IB** (c. 1450 BC) administrative documents baked by destruction fires. This produces a heavy bias toward one period and one site, and any statistical work must correct for it.
- Supports include tablets, roundels, nodules and other sealed documents, about 41 inscribed stone **libation tables** (out of about 1,000 known libation tables from 27 sites), stone and clay vessels, gold and silver pins and rings, and pithoi. A few finds come from outside Crete: Kea, Thera, Melos, Kythera, Laconia, Miletus, Samothrace, Tel Lachish, and possibly Troy and Tel Haror.
- The text count depends on how you count. A 2025 survey (Mavridaki et al., IARAS) refers to 1,644 artefacts in one database and over 1,427 inscriptions. A 2026 open GitHub dataset ("Navarre-AI/linear-a") cites RILA Supplement 1 as counting **1,534 physical inscriptions**. SigLA holds **772 documents** (as crawled by the LogogramNLP 2024 paper, arXiv 2408.04628). A separate 2026 harvest counted **787 inscriptions / 3,097 sign-group passages**. Your project should fix and publish one canonical document list with explicit inclusion rules.

**Key 2024–2026 addition: the Knossos ivory "sceptre" (Anetaki plot)**
- This is an ivory ring and handle from a Neopalatial cult building at Knossos, inscribed on all faces. With about **119 signs** preserved wholly or in part, it is the **longest known Linear A inscription**. It is published as **KN Zg 57 and KN Zg 58** in Kanta, Nakassis, Palaima & Perna (2025), *Ariadne Supplements*, pp. 27–43 (doi 10.26248/ariadne.vi.1841). The ring and handle texts look like the work of different scribes. The excavators call it the "first Linear A economic document coming from a cult building". A full edition is announced from INSTAP Academic Press. Kanta and Perna also presented "New light on the fractional system in the Linear A script" at the 2025 Madrid Mycenological Colloquium. (High confidence on the facts; the interpretation is preliminary.) Note that the object was excavated before 2017 and first presented in 2021, so press reports describing it as a "2024 discovery" are inaccurate.

## 4. Linguistic and script features: what is near-certain, probable or contested

**4.1 Morphology: agglutinative and heavily affixing (Mainstream/Medium-High)**
- Yves Duhoux (1978 onwards, including 1989 on the libation formula) and later Brent Davis showed that sign-groups recur with stable "stems" and variable beginnings and endings. Salgarella 2025 summarises the current view: Minoan "appears to have been an agglutinative language, making copious use of prefixes and suffixes". **-RU and -RE** look like case-type markers. **J-/JA-** is a prefix meaning something like "from/of". **-JA** is a derivational suffix forming adjectives (cf. place-adjectives in -JA such as SE-TO-I-JA). (Wikipedia citing Salgarella 2025, pp. 51–54.)
- Key alternation: **A-SA-SA-RA-ME ~ JA-SA-SA-RA-ME**. Under Linear B values this is /a/ vs /ja/, i.e. a j-prefix on a vowel-initial word. Reading it this way is a Kober-style inference: it treats systematic variation as evidence of inflection or derivation.
- Linear A words appear in Linear B with Greek endings: **QA-QA-RU → *qa-qa-ro***, **DI-DE-RU → *di-de-ro***. This supports both the shared sound values and the idea that -RU is a Minoan ending Greek-speakers remodelled. (High for the correspondences; Medium for the morphological interpretation.)

**4.2 Syntax: verb-initial, probably VSO (Mainstream-but-contested/Medium)**
- **Davis 2013, "Syntax in Linear A: The Word-Order of the 'Libation Formula'", *Kadmos* 52(1):35–52 (De Gruyter, paywalled).** By comparing the order of elements across libation-formula variants, Davis concluded the language is "a non-Indo-European, non-Semitic, verb-initial language, perhaps with VSO word order".
- **R. Thomas 2020, "Some reflections on morphology in the language of the Linear A libation formula", *Kadmos* 59** (author's preprint free online). Thomas accepts VSO, argues for a "complex polysynthetic verb" and heavy prefixing (e.g. a TA-NA- prefix before the verb on three libation tables), and questions Davis's identification of A-SA-SA-RA-ME as a noun.
- The OVS word order has also been proposed; it is a minority view. All syntax conclusions rest on fewer than about 40 formulaic religious texts, so they are hypotheses, not facts.

**4.3 Phonology and vowel frequencies (Contested)**
- **a, i, u** dominate and **o** is rare. Older work (e.g. Packard 1974; the Palmer and Duhoux tradition) took this to mean a three-vowel system /a i u/. Some scholars also argue Minoan had no labio-velars (the q-series) and that the only voiced stop, *d*, was really a dental fricative.
- **Recent revision (2024–2025):** Steele 2024 and Salgarella 2025 question the three-vowel model. The o- and e-signs are supported by Cypriot comparanda and by shared names (PA-I-TO, SE-TO-I-JA). They also question the assumption of purely open syllables, since spellings like PA-I-TO probably hid an unwritten /s/ (cf. *Phaistos*), so Linear A was probably as "defective" (under-representing sounds) as Linear B. **Implication for computation:** vowel-frequency statistics carry information about the *script* as well as the *language*. Any typological comparison must model spelling conventions explicitly.

**4.4 The "libation formula" (High that it is a formula; Low–Medium on meaning)**
- This is a standardised sequence found mainly on stone libation tables from peak and cave sanctuaries (Iouktas, Palaikastro, Kophinas, Apodoulou, Syme and others). It has a fixed structure with variable slots (Duhoux 1989). A typical full form runs: (A-TA-)I-*301-WA-JA | JA-DI-KI-TU / A-DI-KI-TE-TE (toponym slot, probably Dikte) | JA-SA-SA-RA-ME / A-SA-SA-RA-ME | U-NA-KA-NA-SI | I-PI-NA-MA | SI-RU-TE.
- The stem **I-*301** is widely thought to be the verb ("give/dedicate"), on the basis of its position and its variation across variants (Salgarella 2025; Davis 2013). A-SA-SA-RA-ME is often read as a divine name or epithet, but this is unproven. Interpretations such as "mother goddess" (Owens) or Semitic readings (di Mino) are not accepted.
- A similar sequence in Cretan Hieroglyphic, the **"Archanes formula"** (A-SA-SA-RA-ME's Hieroglyphic counterpart is debated), is the main textual link between the two scripts.

**4.5 Number system and fractions (Integers: Consensus. Fraction values: Mainstream-but-refined)**
- Integers are decimal and additive, and sums can be checked. On HT 104, for example, the entries are 45 + J + 20 J· + 29 and the total is 95 (Corazza et al. 2021).
- **Fractions ("klasmatograms").** **Corazza, Ferrara, Montecchi, Tamburini & Valério 2021, "The mathematical values of fraction signs in the Linear A script: A computational, statistical and typological approach", *Journal of Archaeological Science* 125:105214** (repository PDF free at flore.unifi.it). The team (ERC INSCRIBE, Bologna) combined palaeography, constraint programming and statistical optimisation. They proposed **J = 1/2, E = 1/4, B = 1/5, D = 1/6, F = 1/8, K = 1/10, H = 1/16?, L2 = 1/20, A = 1/24?, L3 = 1/30, L4 = 1/40, L6 = 1/60**, with **W = BB? (2/5)** and **X = AA? (1/12)**. Y, Ω and some L-forms occur only once, and "L" may be a spurious sign. Fractions combine by addition: **JE = 3/4, DD = 1/3, EF = 3/8**. Linear B partly supports this: Linear B reused some Linear A fraction signs as sub-units of measure (e.g. DD and AA as 1/3 and 1/12 of the wool unit; K as 1/10 of the dry unit). **This is the best existing model of methodologically sound computational work on Linear A.** Earlier work: Bennett 1950; Facchetti 1994; Montecchi; Schrijver's and others' alternative value sets. Debate continues on the low-frequency signs, and the 2025 Kanta–Perna paper on the sceptre promises "new light".

**4.6 Relations between scripts**
- **Cretan Hieroglyphic** (c. 2100–1700 BC; CHIC 1996). Its sign list contains 96 syllabograms, 33 logograms, 9 klasmatograms, 4 numeral signs and 2 "stiktograms" (signs marking where a sequence begins). It overlapped in time with Linear A, which was earlier favoured in the south (Phaistos) while Hieroglyphic was favoured in the north (Knossos, Malia, Petras). Most specialists think Linear A shares an ancestor with Hieroglyphic or partly derives from it. **Ferrara, Montecchi & Valério 2022 (*Pasiphae*)** and **2023 (*Oxford Journal of Archaeology*, "In search of lost signs")** argue that one Linear A sign can descend from more than one Hieroglyphic antecedent, and they treat (j)a-sa- as a shared prefix. (Mainstream.) Hieroglyphic and Linear A also share words and phrases ("total", place names, personal names) (Karnava 1999).
- **The Arkalochori axe** is a bronze votive double axe with a short incised inscription (GORILA AR Zf 1). It is variously classified as Linear A or Hieroglyphic, and the I-DA-MA-TE reading (I-DA = Ida) is commonly cited but disputed. **The Phaistos Disc** is a separate, stamped, unique object. It is not part of the Linear A corpus and is not a useful computational target (one text, no parallels).
- **Cypro-Minoan** (c. 1550–1050 BC; Cyprus and Ugarit). The corpus is under 300 objects (about 217 documents plus 26 more texts in Ferrara 2012/13). Olivier and Masson divided it into **CM0 (archaic Enkomi tablet ##001), CM1, CM2 (Enkomi long tablets) and CM3 (Ugarit)**, allegedly for different languages. **Recent consensus is moving toward a single script:** Ferrara 2012/13 (same statistical and combinatorial regularities, divisions correlated with writing medium); **Corazza, Tamburini, Valério & Ferrara 2022, PLOS ONE, "Unsupervised deep learning supports reclassification of Bronze age Cypriot writing system"**, which found sign-shape clusters follow the writing medium rather than the subscript; **Valério 2024 (*Pasiphae*), on the phonetic values of Cypro-Minoan signs**; and **Polig & Donnelly 2025 (*SMEA*), "Between Frustration and Progress: An Integrated Cypro-Minoan Signary"**, which concludes that Cypro-Minoan "should be considered a single script". Some still defend CM2 and CM3 as separate scripts. Unicode block U+12F90–U+12FFF was added in Unicode 14.0 (2021).
- **Eteocretan** is the name for a handful of 7th–3rd century BC inscriptions from eastern Crete, chiefly **Praisos** and **Dreros**, written in the Greek alphabet in a non-Greek language. There are about five secure texts (three from Praisos, two from Dreros) plus a few fragments; Duhoux refers to "six known Eteocretan inscriptions". The standard edition is **Y. Duhoux, *L'Étéocrétois: les textes – la langue* (1982)**, with a short update in Duhoux 2007 (in Christidis, *A History of Ancient Greek*). Duhoux compared it with Linear A and found phonetic and morphological differences large enough that he insists on "rigorously separat[ing]" the two lines of study. **Assessment:** Eteocretan is the most plausible *later descendant* of some pre-Greek Cretan language, but there is no demonstrated link to Linear A. The corpus is far too small for statistical comparison. (Medium–Low for any genetic link.)

## 6. Was Minoan Indo-European? Arguments, evidence and who holds what

**6.1 Non-Indo-European / isolate (Mainstream default; Medium-High)**
- **Supporters:** Davis (2013, 2014: "non-Indo-European, non-Semitic, verb-initial"); Duhoux; Salgarella (2025: Minoan "does not appear to be a member of any recognized known language family"); Beekes (pre-Greek substrate as non-Indo-European); most Aegean epigraphers.
- **Arguments:** (i) VSO order and a heavily prefixing, agglutinative morphology are atypical of early Indo-European, especially Anatolian, which is verb-final (SOV) and suffixing. (ii) Stems and affixes do not resemble Indo-European paradigms when read with Linear B values. (iii) Ancient DNA shows steppe-linked ancestry only "appeared in Crete gradually from the seventeenth to twelfth centuries bc" (Skourtanioti et al. 2023), with minimal or none in the earliest (late 17th/16th-century BC) Aposelemis individuals (Section 8). (iv) The Greek pre-Greek substrate has a non-Indo-European phonological profile (Beekes: prenasalisation, a pre-Greek /a/-vowel system, no voicing contrast; Beekes 2014 reports 1,106 words marked "PG" in his *Etymological Dictionary of Greek*).
- **Weakness:** syntax is inferred from about 40 religious texts, and the case for "non-Indo-European" rests partly on that inference. Absence of a match is not proof of non-membership.

**6.2 Anatolian / Luwian (Serious minority; Low-Medium)**
- **Supporters:** L. R. Palmer (1950s–60s: Linear A as Luwian); M. Finkelberg (*Greeks and Pre-Greeks*, 2005: the toponymic suffixes -nth- and -ss- "can now be accounted for as typically Anatolian or, to be more precise, Luwian"); earlier Mellaart-type migration scenarios; F. Woudhuizen (Luwian readings, more idiosyncratic); and some Anatolianists who treat certain pre-Greek words as Anatolian loans (e.g. *dépas* "cup" ~ Hieroglyphic Luwian *tipas*; *tolýpē* "ball of wool" ~ Hittite/Luwian *taluppa*).
- **Against:** Beekes's review of Finkelberg called Palmer's Luwian theory "dated". Anatolian words in Greek can reflect contact across the Aegean rather than a substrate. Anatolian Indo-European is SOV and suffixing, which conflicts with the VSO and prefixing profile. No Luwian paradigm has been found in Linear A.
- **Genetics does not exclude it.** Lazaridis et al. 2022 place the split between Anatolian and the rest of Indo-European in the West Asian highlands, a source *without* steppe ancestry. So an Anatolian-related language could in principle have reached Crete with the Early Bronze Age "eastern" (Anatolian) gene flow that Skourtanioti et al. 2023 detected. This keeps the Anatolian hypothesis *testable*, and it is the only Indo-European option that genetics leaves open.

**6.3 Greek (Fringe/Rejected for Linear A)**
- The proposals include Linear A as an archaic form of Greek, and the prediction that the Knossos sceptre will "lead to a rapid reappraisal" toward Greek (an Academia.edu essay). They fail on three counts: Greek morphology is absent; Linear A words reappear in Linear B *remodelled with Greek endings* (QA-QA-RU → *qa-qa-ro*), which shows they were foreign to Greek; and Minoans lacked steppe ancestry.

**6.4 Indo-Iranian (Fringe)**
- Proposed by a few amateurs and outsiders. It has no academic support, and genetics (no steppe ancestry, which is a hallmark of Indo-Iranian speakers) argues strongly against it.

**6.5 Semitic (Fringe in its strong form; Low)**
- **C. H. Gordon** (1957–1966, *Evidence for the Minoan Language*) equated KU-RO with Semitic *kull-* "all/total" and proposed further matches. **Jan Best** later offered Phoenician-style readings. The idea has revived repeatedly, most recently with **T. di Mino 2026** (Section 7.4). **Against:** Davis's syntactic profile (VSO is compatible with Semitic, but the prefixing system does not resemble Semitic root-and-pattern morphology as written); the matches are small in number and selective; and Gordon's lexical matches "lacked grammatical grounding". **What survives:** individual *loanwords* between Crete and the Levant (e.g. commodity terms, cumin/sesame-type Wanderwörter in Linear B) are plausible. A genetic relationship of Minoan to Semitic is not.

**6.6 Tyrsenian (Etruscan–Lemnian–Rhaetic) (Minority hypothesis; Low-Medium)**
- **G. M. Facchetti** (*Kadmos* 40, 2001; *Appunti di morfologia etrusca* 2002) and **Facchetti & M. Negri** (*Creta Minoica*, Olschki 2003) proposed that Minoan is the ancestor or a sister of Eteocretan and Proto-Tyrsenian. The Tyrsenian family itself was defined by H. Rix (1998). Facchetti himself presents it as a hypothesis. A 2024 MDPI review calls it disputed. **It is attractive** because Tyrsenian is non-Indo-European, Lemnian is attested in the Aegean, and both have agglutinative suffixes. **Weaknesses:** Etruscan is suffixing and, as far as is known, not VSO, and the proposed lexical matches are few.

**6.7 Hurrian, Hattic, Kartvelian, Uralic and others**
- **Hurrian and Hattic:** occasionally invoked for typological reasons (ergativity, prefixing in Hattic). There is no systematic case, so Low. **Uralic/Finno-Ugric** (Revesz, 2017 onward): Fringe (Section 7.4).

## 7. Computational, statistical and other objective analyses: sound versus flawed

**7.1 Methodologically sound, widely respected**
- **Packard 1974, *Minoan Linear A*** (and Packard 1971, *Kadmos* 10, "Computer techniques in the study of the Minoan Linear Script A"). This was the first computer concordance with sign-frequency and positional statistics, and an estimate of how far Linear A sound values diverged from Linear B (about 10.8 ± 1.8%). It is still a valuable baseline. (Print only.)
- **Arithmetic and accounting analyses:** Bennett (1950) on fractions; Schoep (2002) on administration; Younger's and Montecchi's checks of KU-RO totals; **Corazza et al. 2021** on fractions (constraint programming plus statistics plus typology). This category is the most robust: the ground truth (the arithmetic) does not depend on knowing the language.
- **Palaeographic databases and sign-relation work:** **Salgarella 2020** (Linear A to Linear B sign transmission), **SigLA** (Salgarella & Castellan), **Steele** (writing practice), and **Ferrara, Montecchi & Valério** (Hieroglyphic–Linear A palaeography, 2022–2023), all within **ERC INSCRIBE** (Bologna, PI S. Ferrara, "Invention of Scripts and their Beginnings").
- **Morphological and syntactic analyses by hand:** Duhoux (prefixes, suffixes and the libation formula), **Davis 2013/2014**, Thomas 2020. These are qualitative but explicit, and they are ideal targets to formalise and test statistically.
- **Neural work on Cypro-Minoan:** Corazza et al. 2022 (PLOS ONE). This is unsupervised deep learning on sign images, used to test a hypothesis about the script (one script or three?), not to "decipher". It is a model of how to use machine learning correctly.

**7.2 Machine-learning decipherment of *known* scripts (sound; tells us what is possible)**
- **Snyder, Barzilay & Knight 2010** (ACL). On Ugaritic→Hebrew, a Bayesian model mapped 29/30 letters correctly and found the right Hebrew cognate for 60% of Ugaritic words that have one.
- **Luo, Cao & Barzilay 2019, "Neural Decipherment via Minimum-Cost Flow: From Ugaritic to Linear B"** (ACL; arXiv 1906.06718). The model "correctly translates 67.3% of cognates" of Linear B into Greek and reports "a 5.5% absolute improvement over state-of-the-art results" on Ugaritic. Its authors stress that it depends on knowing the right related language and on large cognate overlap, and that "the reduced decipherment model fails" without linguistic priors. The paper does not attempt Linear A.
- **Luo, Hartmann, Santus, Barzilay & Cao 2021** (TACL, "Deciphering Undersegmented Ancient Scripts Using Phonetic Prior"). It tested on Ugaritic, Gothic and Iberian and proposed a **language-closeness measure** that correctly identified related languages for Gothic and Ugaritic. This is the most relevant published tool for "which family?" questions. Its power at Linear A's size (a few thousand tokens, mostly names and logograms) has not been demonstrated.
- **LogogramNLP 2024** (arXiv 2408.04628) used 772 SigLA tablets for findspot classification. This is a useful demonstration that SigLA can be machine-processed, not a linguistic result.

**7.3 Mixed or weak**
- **Nepal & Perono Cacciafoco 2024, "Minoan Cryptanalysis", *Information* 15(2):73** (MDPI, open access), plus the earlier NTU "brute force attack" papers (Loh & Perono Cacciafoco; Eu et al. 2019) and **Manoj & Perono Cacciafoco 2025**. These compare sign shapes (Linear A against the Carian alphabet and the Cypriot syllabary) and match Linear A words against word lists of many languages. Of the 2024 paper's visual-similarity matches, it says "only … 43 matches" were produced. The authors themselves warn that a high frequency of matches "would not imply the correctness of the interpretation". **Assessment:** transparent, but without proper null models and with shape-similarity assumptions that are hard to defend across unrelated scripts. Treat as exploratory.
- **Mavridaki et al. 2025** (IARAS), "A Machine-Readable Dictionary for the Corpus of Linear-A". Useful as a resource survey; not a linguistic result.

**7.4 Fringe or flawed claimed decipherments (2017–2026)**
- **Peter Z. Revesz (Univ. of Nebraska–Lincoln): Uralic/"West-Ugric" Minoan.** Works include *WSEAS Trans. Inf. Sci. Appl.* 14 (2017):306–335, which used visual sign similarity to Old Hungarian and other alphabets to build a new "phonetic grid", then translated 28 Linear A texts and one Eteocretan text as "All cave spirits", "queen", etc. Later work includes IEEE "Minoan and Finno-Ugric Regular Sound Changes Discovered by Data Mining" and **Revesz 2024, *Information* 15:646, "Archaeogenetic Data Mining Supports a Uralic–Minoan Homeland in the Danube Basin"** (G25 admixture plus a few SNPs and haplogroups). **Problems:** it discards the Linear B values (the best-supported part of the field); judging sign similarity by shape across unrelated scripts is unconstrained; it has no null model; it mostly appears in low-selectivity venues; and the genetics contradicts the mainstream aDNA picture (Minoans as about 75%+ Anatolian/Aegean Neolithic farmers, with no steppe or hunter-gatherer influx). **Not accepted by Aegeanists or Uralicists.** (Fringe.)
- **Gareth Owens** (Hellenic Mediterranean University, Crete). He has published sound-value comparisons (Owens 1996/1999, the 9–13% divergence estimate) and has publicised readings of libation-formula terms as references to a "mother goddess", leaning toward an Indo-European affinity. **Reception:** the comparative-value work is cited; the semantic readings are not accepted. (Low confidence on the readings.)
- **Tom di Mino, "Ya Diktu: Grammar of the Minoan Peak Sanctuary Libation Formula" (2026).**
  - **Timeline:** announced June 2026 (a 9-page draft, a 408-entry lexicon, 40 sign values including 13 new ones, built with Claude Code-generated Python over GORILA/SigLA). A 41-page version is dated August 2026. A **pre-print appeared in September 2026 (Zenodo DOI 10.5281/zenodo.22730321, CC BY 4.0)** claiming Minoan is "Tinitic", an independent branch of Central Semitic, with a 511-entry lexicon in five confidence tiers and 463 "translated" inscriptions.
  - **The key move:** assign *301 = *na*, read I-*301 as from a root *n-w-y* "dwell", and match grammar to Semitic prefixes, reflexives and case endings.
  - **Reception:** no peer-reviewed endorsement. The languagehat discussion (July–August 2026) recalled that Gordon's KU-RO = *kullu* "proved" nothing. An independent GitHub project ("linear-a-check", kbennett2000) is testing the rules cycle by cycle. In its cycle 9 it asks whether readings survive once rules written specifically for them are switched off; at the time of this survey it draws no conclusion. **The user's own null-model result** (root-matching scores real Hebrew no better than random consonant lexicons) is a decisive methodological objection. With three-consonant roots, few constraints and flexible vowel and spelling rules, matches are cheap. (Fringe; **reject as a decipherment** until it beats a pre-registered null.)
- **General diagnostic** for the whole category: the method adds language-specific rules after seeing the data; it lacks held-out tests; it cannot recover a known decipherment (Linear B→Greek) under the same rules; and it does not compare against random or unrelated control lexicons.

## 8. Archaeogenetics: Neolithic to Bronze Age Crete, Cyprus, Anatolia and the mainland

| Study | Key finding | Confidence / access |
|---|---|---|
| **Lazaridis et al. 2017, *Nature* 548:214–218** ("Genetic origins of the Minoans and Mycenaeans") | 19 individuals (10 Minoans). Minoans and Mycenaeans had "at least three-quarters of their ancestry from the first Neolithic farmers of western Anatolia and the Aegean, and most of the remainder from ancient populations related to those of the Caucasus and Iran". **Only Mycenaeans** had an additional "northern" (steppe-related) component of about 4–16% | High. Paywalled at Nature; author PDF on the Reich lab site |
| **Clemente et al. 2021, *Cell* 184(10):2565–2586** ("The genomic history of the Aegean palatial civilizations"; doi 10.1016/j.cell.2021.03.039) | EBA Minoan (Petras), Cycladic (Koufonisi) and Helladic (Manika) genomes "are homogeneous and derive most of their ancestry from Neolithic Aegeans", shaped by "relatively small-scale migration from East of the Aegean" (Caucasus-related). MBA northern Greeks (Elati-Logkas) show "~50% Pontic-Caspian Steppe-related ancestry, dated at ca. 2,600–2,000 BCE" | High; open access |
| **Lazaridis et al. 2022, *Science* 377:eabm4247** ("The genetic history of the Southern Arc") plus two companion papers | More than 700 genomes. Eastern hunter-gatherer/steppe ancestry is "absent in Minoan individuals of Crete", and present in most Mycenaeans (about 9% Yamnaya-type). Anatolian Indo-European speakers probably descend from a West Asian highland source *without* steppe ancestry, so the link between Anatolian and the rest of Indo-European lies in the highlands | High; author PDFs free |
| **Skourtanioti et al. 2023, *Nature Ecology & Evolution* 7:290–303** (doi 10.1038/s41559-022-01952-3) | 102 individuals (66 from Crete). Neolithic Cretans resemble other Neolithic Aegeans. The end of the Neolithic and the EBA saw "eastern" gene flow, "predominantly of Anatolian origin in Crete". Central/Eastern European (steppe-linked) ancestry "appeared in Crete gradually from the seventeenth to twelfth centuries BC, a period when the influence of the mainland over the island intensified". The authors also report "consanguineous endogamy practiced at high frequencies, unprecedented in the global ancient DNA record" | High; open access |
| **Yediay et al. 2024, bioRxiv 2024.12.02.626332** ("Ancient genomics support deep divergence between Eastern and Western Mediterranean Indo-European languages"; 314 new genomes, 224 strontium analyses) | Bronze Age Cyprus was a "genetic and cultural 'melting pot'". MBA–LBA Cypriots resemble Lebanese and Eastern Anatolian Bronze Age people. A few individuals from Hala Sultan Tekke and Lapithos carry Late Helladic/Mycenaean-like steppe ancestry, and one Lapithos individual from about 4,100–4,000 BP already clusters with LBA Greeks. Iron Age Cypriots are more uniform, with a small Yamnaya (Greek-derived) share. Steppe ancestry in Greece is Yamnaya-derived, unlike the Bell Beaker-mediated steppe ancestry of Italy and Iberia | **Medium: still an unreviewed preprint as of late 2026** (no journal version found) |

**What this means for the language question**
1. **Steppe-associated Indo-European (Greek, and a fortiori Indo-Iranian) is very unlikely for Neopalatial Minoan.** Steppe ancestry reached Crete only gradually from the 17th century BC, which matches the Mycenaean takeover and the switch to Linear B, not the creation of Linear A. (High.)
2. **Minoan most plausibly continues either the Neolithic Aegean farmer language(s) or a language brought with the late Neolithic/EBA Anatolian gene flow.** The first option implies non-Indo-European. The second could be non-Indo-European (Hattic-like, or something unknown) or, less likely given the typology, Anatolian Indo-European, since Anatolian Indo-European plausibly spread from a population without steppe ancestry. (Medium.)
3. **Cyprus:** the Levantine/East-Anatolian profile of MBA–LBA Cypriots is consistent with Cypro-Minoan writing one or more *non-Minoan* local languages (e.g. "Eteocypriot"-type) on an Aegean-derived script. The Mycenaean-affiliated outliers fit the later Arcado-Cypriot Greek. (Medium; preprint.)
4. Genes are not languages. These are priors, not proofs, and the user's project should treat them as such.

## 9. Recommendations: where a solid early computational result is most achievable

Ranked by how achievable each is and how falsifiable the result would be:

1. **Build a canonical, versioned corpus with a published schema** (SigLA plus GORILA IDs plus Younger readings, with document-level inclusion rules, LM IB/site metadata and uncertainty flags). The NC-SA licence permits academic redistribution of derived data under the same terms. Without this, no result is reproducible. (Effort: weeks.)
2. **Accounting-constraint analysis (highest probability of a solid result).** Formalise every list with KU-RO, KI-RO or PO-TO-KU-RO as an integer and fraction constraint system. (a) Re-estimate fraction values with Corazza et al.'s method on the larger and corrected corpus, including the sceptre once it is published. (b) Quantify how many totals are consistent under the alternative value sets. (c) Test whether KI-RO entries behave arithmetically like deficits. The predictions are falsifiable by arithmetic.
3. **Affix discovery against null models.** Use unsupervised segmentation (MDL/Morfessor-style or Bayesian) to recover prefixes and suffixes (J-/A-, -JA, -RU/-RE, TA-NA-). Compare the results against corpora shuffled within documents and syllable-permuted, and against Linear B processed the same way (where the Greek morphology is known). A result such as "-JA is a productive suffix at p < 0.001 under null X" would be a genuine, citable advance on Duhoux and Davis.
4. **Distributional sign-value testing.** Use positional and alternation statistics to (a) test the homomorphy–homophony principle sign by sign (e.g. do the AB signs behave in Linear A the way their Linear B values predict in shared names?), and (b) cluster undetermined signs (*301, *118 etc.) by context. Then make held-out predictions (e.g. "*301 patterns with CV signs of vowel X"), which can be tested on new texts such as the Knossos sceptre.
5. **"Decipherability power analysis" (the most original contribution, and it extends the user's null-model work).** Take known pairs (Linear B→Greek via DAMOS; Ugaritic→Hebrew; Iberian test sets from Luo et al. 2021). Degrade them to Linear A conditions: 7,362–7,396 sign tokens (the Linear A Digital Corpus count), name-heavy, a defective spelling rule, about 10–14% sound-value noise, and fragmentary text. Then measure whether Luo-style models, root matchers and similarity metrics can still (i) identify the right family above random and unrelated-language baselines and (ii) recover correct cognates. **If no method succeeds at Linear A's scale, that is itself a publishable negative result**, and it would let reviewers dismiss future claims (di Mino, Revesz, Gordon-style) by a quantitative standard. The user's Hebrew-vs-random-lexicon test is a prototype; generalise it into an open benchmark and pre-register the evaluation.
6. **Typological profiling under script uncertainty.** Estimate the vowel inventory and phonotactics (e.g. whether the rarity of *o* reflects the language or the script) by fitting generative models of a "defective syllabary" to Linear B (where the answer is known) and then to Linear A. Report credible intervals, not point claims.
7. **Script-relation analyses** (Hieroglyphic → Linear A; Linear A → Cypro-Minoan) using palaeographic embeddings on SigLA tracings, following Corazza et al. 2022. Achievable, but it depends on image licensing.

**Do not** make "identify the language family" the Phase 2 target until step 5 shows that it is statistically possible.

## 10. Caveats and open questions

- **Corpus skew:** most text is LM IB Hagia Triada administration. Patterns found may be scribal or site conventions, not features of the language.
- **One language or several?** Not settled (Salgarella 2025; Davis). Methods should allow for mixtures, e.g. administrative versus religious registers.
- **The sound values are an assumption.** Every phonological inference inherits roughly 10–15% divergence uncertainty and the problem of defective spelling.
- **Sources flagged as secondhand or unreviewed:** the SigLA licence (reported by a third party); the Yediay et al. Cyprus results (preprint); document counts from GitHub datasets; di Mino materials (self-published); press accounts of the sceptre (dating errors). **Paywalled key sources:** Davis 2013 (*Kadmos*), Salgarella 2020 and 2025 (CUP), GORILA and CHIC in print, Lazaridis 2017 (*Nature*; author copy available). **Open:** Corazza et al. 2021 (repository PDF), Corazza et al. 2022 (PLOS ONE), Clemente 2021, Skourtanioti 2023, Braović 2024, Luo et al. 2019/2021, Nepal & Perono Cacciafoco 2024.
- **Open questions suited to falsifiable computational work:** fraction values for the rare signs; the semantics of KI-RO; how productive the affixes are; the values of *301 and other Linear A-only signs; whether some sign-groups are number words; whether the libation formula varies by region or by syntax; whether Cypro-Minoan is one script and how its values relate to Linear A; and whether *any* family-identification method has the power to work at this corpus size.
