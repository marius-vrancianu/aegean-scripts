## Result: outcome B, Test 1 only — the prediction is met

English verbs beat the matched pseudo-lexicons on Linear A (Test 1), but by no more than they beat them on Greek words (Test 2). English is not related to the language of Linear A, so the Test 1 result is a false positive, and Test 2 removed it.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Test 1**, Linear A, 414 sign-groups | 0.705 | 0.641 (0.582–0.693) | +0.065 | **p1 = 0.009** |
| Greek control, 902 words | 0.490 | 0.420 (0.376–0.460) | +0.070 | 0.002 |
| Greek control at Linear A's lengths | | | +0.081 | |
| **Test 2**, Linear A minus control | | (−0.044 to +0.044) | **−0.016** | **p2 = 0.754** |

- Pair p 0.754; p1 ≤ α and p2 > α, so the outcome is B ("above the null, but no more than on the Greek control"). The registered prediction was p2 > α (outcome A, or B with p1 ≤ α only): **met**.
- **In words.** Of 414 Linear A sign-groups, M1 finds an English verb "root" for 292 (70.5%). With a random lexicon of the same size it finds one for 64.1% on average, about 27 fewer. Only 8 of the 1,000 random lexicons did as well as English. On its own, Test 1 would have reported English in Linear A at p = 0.009. English does at least as well against the same random lexicons on Greek words, so Test 2 says the advantage has nothing to do with Linear A.

- **Reading (confidence high for the figures; the explanation is my reasoning, rule 5):** English's advantage on Linear A is carried by its two-letter forms. The matched null under-draws them (79 in English against a null mean of 61.7, disclosed in H-A5-02 before the run), and M1 reads many short sign-groups as two-consonant skeletons. Without those forms English is level with the pseudo-lexicons on Linear A.
- **Leftover bias (review note N7):** without two-letter forms every pseudo-lexicon is larger than the real lexicon (936 against 939 to 963; mean 953.3), which lowers the real lexicon's excess slightly. Near the real size the lexicon-size curve below gains about 0.15–0.17 in hit rate per doubling; 1.8% more forms (0.027 doublings) is worth about 0.004–0.005, small beside the change from +0.065 to −0.007. The direction overstates the share of the effect that the two-letter forms carry, but not enough to change the reading.
- On the control, about half of English's advantage survives without the two-letter forms (+0.032). So English differs from its pseudo-lexicons in more than form length, and on Greek words that difference helps it. Whether this is shared Indo-European root shape (the bias H-A5-02 named in advance) cannot be told from this run.

## What follows
- **Test 1 alone gives false positives.** For an unrelated language it passed at p = 0.009, stably across sources and resampling. A Tier 1 result must never be quoted from Test 1 alone; this is what Test 2 is for, and here it worked.
- **The matched null's length bias is real and large enough to matter** for lexicons with short forms. A length-stratified matched null (forms drawn per length to the real count at each length after expansion) is a candidate for a revised protocol, registered as a new hypothesis; H-A5-01's protocol stays as it is.
- **What this pair does not show:** that Test 2's assumption holds in general. English is Indo-European like Greek, and any shared root shape would push towards outcome A (stated in H-A5-02 in advance). A non-Indo-European unrelated language is the stricter check.
- This outcome says nothing about the language of Linear A. English was never a candidate.
